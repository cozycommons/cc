
## Bath

The five CSVs come from separate experiments. Digitizing errors in their headers describe reading the published figures. Replicate variation, probe placement and the choice of immersion time can contribute larger errors.

- **Akmal, brisket.** Figure 4.5 shows means and standard deviations for three 25 × 30 × 100 mm brisket pieces, with a Maxim iButton (specified accuracy ±0.5 °C) at the geometric center. The CSV uses the open circles. The nominal 4 °C start appears as about 3.1 °C on the graph. Use both short dimensions for heat transfer. The authors' 30-minute result applies to their 71 °C pilot. They assumed the same come-up time for other treatments. [Akmal, Massey University (2024), §§4.2.3–4.2.5](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/6af359df-ffd9-4cc8-a2a9-32a795a13f68/content)
- **DeVane, strip loin.** Figure 1 shows representative curves for the 25.4 mm frozen-cut target. The CSV uses the gray SV30 curve from the 72.5 °C Anova bath, including its approach to equilibrium. A copper–constantan thermocouple ran through foam tape to the center and fed an Omega HH520 logger. The thesis gives neither replicate count for this particular trace nor individual thawed dimensions. It plots the first point at minute 1, at 19.7 °C despite overnight thawing at 4 °C. The CSV treats that point as time zero (possible one-minute offset). The four hold treatments share one setup. [DeVane, University of Georgia (2023), pp. 38–40, 62](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/3010)
- **Fareh, eye of round.** Figure 4-1 shows a center iButton trace and a dashed bath trace for nominally 60 × 60 × 100 mm pieces. The plot's first seven minutes precede the first recorded bath temperature. The CSV uses that first bath reading as the best available immersion marker, when the core reads 4.1 °C. Taking the graph origin instead would add seven minutes and give a start near 3 °C. The stirred bath was preheated to 64 °C and reset to 60 °C on loading. The number of samples represented by the curve is unspecified. Fareh reports higher cook loss in the instrumented pieces and difficulty centering the logger (p. 75). [Fareh, Massey University (2018), pp. 72–75](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/server/api/core/bitstreams/362d38be-a407-4f4c-81b0-c4d3ade5ced0/content)
- **Ishiwatari, beef round.** Figure 6-6 reports three laboratory determinations with center and pouch-surface thermocouples (T-type, 1 mm). Mean initial dimensions were 57.4 × 71.0 × 107 mm, with SDs of 2.35, 1.50 and 3.21 mm. The roast was browned, chilled for an hour, then bagged. A uniform starting temperature is therefore an approximation. The open circles and diamonds give center and film-surface temperatures. The text reports removal at 58 °C after 39.7 ± 1.39 min. The CSV ends with that reported mean pull time. Later figure markers include the 2 °C water chill. Use 57 °C as the target-minus-one threshold for this 58 °C pull, or report the independent 58 °C timing. Figure 6-3 used a steam oven. [Ishiwatari, Tokyo University of Marine Science and Technology (2014), pp. 132–141](https://oacis.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/996/files/kad325.pdf)
- **Logsdon, thick steak.** One probe cook, with a 2.5-inch fridge-slab chart entry beside the photographed steak. Slide 26 gives times for whole-degree Fahrenheit readings over the last three hours. The table records the 130-to-131 °F increment as 56 min 39 s. Probe type, exact placement, cut, lateral dimensions and bath calibration are unknown. Logsdon suspects the probe was off center. The initial temperature and nominal bath in the header are estimates. Compare as a late-cook sensitivity check. [Logsdon, article](https://www.amazingfoodmadeeasy.com/info/modernist-cooking-blog/more/sous-vide-summit-heating-meat) · [Original table](https://s3.amazonaws.com/com.niches.production/story_images/new_images/000/005/192/width_1200/How-Meat-Heats-Sous-Vide-26.jpeg)

**Endpoint-only evidence.** Tkacz, Nogalski and Modzelewska-Kapituła report a preliminary check in which 25 ± 2 mm, 120 ± 5 g semimembranosus steaks reached a reported 60 °C core in about 30–35 min. The method used individual vacuum pouches separated on racks in a 60.0 °C Hendi GN 2/3 bath without active circulation. The study thawed steaks at 4 ± 1 °C for 48 h. The preliminary check has no published starting core temperature, probe specification, sampling interval, endpoint tolerance, replicate count or time series. The 198 sous-vide steaks belong to the tenderness experiment. Continuous electronic core probing is explicitly described for the separate 80 °C conventional cook. The 30–35 min range therefore supplies a timing cross-check only. [Tkacz et al., Applied Sciences 15, 12282 (2025), §§2.3, 2.4.5, 2.5.2 and 4.2](https://doi.org/10.3390/app152212282)

## Sear and rest

Ten CSVs from two sources. One is an instrumented laboratory pan cook from raw; the other is a blog's single thermometer readings after a sear. No measured time series of a steak resting in air after a sear was found in a form the model can rebuild (see "Looked for and not usable").

The sear and rest files carry extra header lines that `validate.py` reads: `schedule` (minutes per step, with the bath temperature or the face on the pan), `air_c`, and `reading_time: unknown` where the source does not say when the core was read.

- **Moya et al., pan-cooked loin (`sear_moya2021_*`, five files).** Longissimus dorsi pieces 19, 26 and 34 mm thick (each ± 2 mm), 26 ± 1 mm wide and 81 ± 21 mm long, started at 20 °C on a non-stick pan held at 215 ± 3 °C by an induction hob's frying sensor (pan surface checked with a Testo 875-2 infrared camera). One face down for two thirds of the time, turned once. Core by a 1.5 mm type T penetration thermocouple and a Testo 177-T4 logger; the authors put the tip's position at ± 1 mm. Six pieces per thickness and doneness; the plotted curve is their mean and the grey band their standard deviation (2 to 3 °C). End-of-cook cores stated in the abstract for 19 mm: 30 ± 3 °C at 180 s, 44 ± 3 °C at 300 s, 57 ± 2 °C at 420 s. The CSVs are the red measured curves of Figs. 6, 8c and 9c read by eye from a 300 dpi render; the embedded figures are blurry rasters, so allow ± 1.5 °C and ± 10 s on top of the paper's own band. The 26 mm (720 s) and 34 mm (770 s) cook times were chosen by the authors to match the 19 mm weight loss. No rest was measured. No surface temperature was measured: the 120 °C contact-face temperature and the 120 W/(m² K) pan-to-meat conductance in the paper come out of the authors' model, fitted to these curves, not from a sensor. Suspicious: the 34 mm core climbs as fast as the 26 mm one and flattens near 58 °C about 150 s after the turn while the pan is still on. A core at mid-thickness should keep rising, so the tip was probably nearer the first face, or the piece (34 mm tall on a 26 mm wide base) did not sit as described. Treat the 34 mm file as weak. The pieces are narrow strips, not steaks, and lost 4 to 12 % of their weight as water, which the conduction model ignores. [Moya, Lorente-Bailo, Salvador, Ferrer-Mairal, Martínez, Calvo and Grasa, J. Food Eng. (2021)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0260877421000236) · [accepted manuscript](https://zaguan.unizar.es/record/110768/files/texto_completo.pdf)
- **Stefan's Gourmet, sous vide then sear (`sear_stefan2020_*`, four files, and `rest_stefan2020_fillet40_chilled`).** One steak per file, one reading each, quoted in the post's text in whole degrees. Ribeye "approximately 2 cm" (148 g and 151 g weighed) and fillet "approximately 4 cm" ("about 150 grams"), baths at 47, 50 or 52 °C (2 h stated for 52 °C), seared 1 minute per side in clarified butter in a "very hot" pan, turned once. Readings after the sear: 60 °C (ribeye from a 52 °C bath), 60 °C (ribeye from 47 °C), 52 °C (fillet from 50 °C), 45 °C (ribeye seared from the refrigerator), 41 °C (fillet seared from "chilled fully", then 5 minutes in foil). Not stated: the thermometer, where its tip sat, the pan temperature, how long after the sear each reading was taken, the refrigerated starting temperatures, and the bath time for the 47 and 50 °C cooks. Suspicious: the same sear gave the same 60 °C from 47 °C and from 52 °C baths, which cannot both be core temperatures unless the reading is of the hot outer layers. In a 20 mm steak a hand probe a few millimetres off centre, or one that averages along its tip, reads the seared layers. The two chilled cooks need more heat than any contact in the test table delivers from an assumed 5 °C start, so the start was warmer, the probe was off centre, or both. The post's own out-of-the-bag reading (43 °C from a 52 °C bath) shows the thermometer lagging by several degrees. Use these as rough checks of direction and size only. The tap-water-chilled ribeye (45 °C after the sear) is not in a CSV: water temperature and chilled core are unknown. [van Gaal, 12 September 2020](https://stefangourmet.com/2020/09/12/the-use-of-chilling-after-cooking-steak-sous-vide/)

**Cross-checks on the pan contact, not validation data.** These are measured or fitted coefficients between a hot plate and meat, defined against the plate's temperature (150 to 215 °C), not against a 100 °C surface as the shipped model does. None was read in full for this work except Moya et al. and Thiffeault.

- Moya et al. (above): 120 W/(m² K) toward a 215 °C pan, with evaporation and drip removing heat at the face; fitted to their core curves.
- Zorrilla and Singh, as quoted by Thiffeault, "The mathematics of burger flipping" (2022), p. 2: 900 W/(m² °C) at the plate under a beef patty, 60 W/(m² °C) on the open top. A model input, relayed second-hand. [arXiv:2206.13900](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13900)
- Wichchukit, Zorrilla and Singh (2001), clam-shell grill and beef patties: 250 to 650 W/(m² °C) from measured plate temperature, patty surface temperature and heat flux, peaking early in the cook and falling to a plateau. Known here only from the abstract and search summaries. [LWT 34(3)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0023643801908813)
- Vine et al. (2019/2020), contact heating: 512 ± 12 W/(m² K) with oil and 198 ± 6 without. Search summary only; the product heated was not confirmed. The open copy refused automated reading. [HAL](https://hal.inrae.fr/hal-02959920v1)

**Looked for and not usable.**

- ThermoWorks, "Carryover Cooking" (blog): oven-roasted pieces pulled at 140 °F and probed while resting on an uncovered sheet pan. A 260 g beef chunk from a 300 °F oven peaked at 148.4 °F (+4.7 °C) and a 286 g one from 425 °F at 153.2 °F (+7.3 °C). Thickness, shape, cook time and the time of the peak are not given and the rest curves are images, so the cook cannot be rebuilt. It does show carryover scaling with the oven's temperature, which is what the model's surface-to-core gradient predicts. [ThermoWorks](https://blog.thermoworks.com/beef/carryover-cooking/)
- Refused automated reading, so unread: AmazingRibs' carryover article, HowToBBQRight's "Meat Carryover Experiment" (a ribeye resting, reported second-hand as gaining about 14 °F in 3 minutes), and the HAL copy of Vine et al.
- No numbers in the text: ThermoWorks' thick-versus-thin steak post (results are in a video), Combustion Inc's carryover help pages, Serious Eats' resting articles as surfaced by search. No published Meater or Hestan Cue log was found.
- USDA ARS belt-grill protocol: the core of a 25 mm steak peaks about 2 minutes after leaving the grill. A timing note without a rise or a temperature series. [ARS shear force procedures](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/30400510/protocols/shearforceprocedures.pdf)
